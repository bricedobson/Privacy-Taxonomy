\begin{abstract}
    \textbf{TODO:} Additional results/findings to be added as work progresses. \newline The W3C Data Privacy Vocabulary (DPV) is the broadest and most comprehensive ontology for describing the domain of personal data processing in a regulatory context. However, as an ontology, it stops short of defining mandatory characteristics such as record structures and typing, attribute-key relationships, and cardinality; leaving these deferred to individual implementations. We examine these by analyzing and reconciling dozens of subdomain-specific taxonomies, producing a reference implementation of the DPV as a comprehensive organization-level data model for privacy. In doing so, we surface latent data relationships as well as the design decisions, considerations, and implementation issues implicit in the DPV. We then consider how these data structures may evolve as an organization matures its privacy practices, using the Carnegie Mellon Maturity Model Integration (CMMI) as a grounding framework. 
    
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\rhostart{o}rganizations, especially larger ones with multi-jurisdictional privacy compliance imperatives, implicitly manage many seemingly disparate categories of privacy-relevant information. Examples include data flows and inventories, processes/activities, purposes and lawful bases, notices, consent records, invocation of individual rights, compliance and risk assessments, and more. Many taxonomies, frameworks, and operational models exist for individual privacy task domains (referred to here as subdomains). The most foundational of these is the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C) Data Privacy Vocabulary (DPV), but as an ontology fundamentally, it leaves significant room for misapplication by implementers who may fail to consider the latent data relationships that are required to be made explicit during operationalization. Luckily, there are hundreds of reference taxonomies for specific domains within data privacy published by academic researchers, international standards organizations, and implicitly within commercial products. While these often have higher normalization and robustness due to their necessity for applied use, interoperability in adjacent processes is almost never addressed within these but remains an eventuality organizations must face as they seek to improve maturity, where these organizations design and operate internal controls within these privacy domains in their efforts to comply with increasingly stringent and detailed legal requirements. Ultimately, this is a problem of semantic compatibility and inadequate contextualization with increase of scope, where disconnected taxonomies produce inconsistent terminology and data, duplication without clear authority, and unrecognized or unclear relationships. 
\\
As a result, organizations meeting this profile often rely on the subsequent data to draw conclusions in contexts where that data is not sufficiently precise, normalized, or complete for the purpose. This creates both operational and legal compliance challenges such as exceeding purpose specification which erodes lawful bases of processing, inadequate recognition and fulfillment of individual rights, and missed opportunity through poor application of legal requirements.
\\
Altogether, this highlights a condition faced by almost all large organizations in this space today, where the volume and importance of privacy-relevant data is increasing based on external growth and internal investment within the domain, but there is an industry-wide maturity gap where fragmented and tool-bound processes lead to fragmented data faster than organizations can normalize the data relationships to make control capabilities legally reliable and defensible. In summary, the problem is created at the intersection of all the following conditions:

\subsection{Identification of Input Taxonomies}
    Input taxonomies were identified through targeted searches of published independent standards, academic papers, as implemented in commercial tools within that specific subdomain. For each subdomain, a target of the three highest quality taxonomies was sought. Quality was evaluated relatively as a function of breadth, or number of topics within the subdomain explicitly accounted for, and readiness for implementation, that is, the apparent presence and completeness of likely required attributes like relevant typing, attribute-key relationships, and consideration for operationally relevant metadata.

\section{Proposed Solution}
    This is a complex problem addressable only at the organization level that ultimately relies on responsible individuals implementing organizational data models with appropriate contextualization, there is no bypass of this. The approach represents a distillation of common structurally-grounded issues and considerations in an accessible place, with the hope that they may be more readily found and applied by practitioners. The idea is that there is value in the DPV as a framework to interconnect otherwise disparate domain taxonomies and a forced mapping of a high-quality subset of the hundreds of these would yield considerations, decision points, and conflicts that the DPV is largely silent on from the implementer perspective. Thus, if these are known and can be made explicit, errors in the application of data models can be reduced through planning and decision making during organizational implementations.
