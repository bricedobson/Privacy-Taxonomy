\section*{Problem Statement}
 
Organizations, especially larger ones with multi-jurisdictional privacy
compliance imperatives, implicitly manage many seemingly disparate
categories of privacy-relevant information. Examples include data flows
and inventories, processes/activities, purposes and lawful bases,
notices, consent records, invocation of individual rights, compliance
and risk assessments, and more. Many taxonomies, frameworks, and
operational models exist for individual privacy task domains. The most
foundational of these is the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C) Data Privacy
Vocabulary (DPV), but as an ontology fundamentally, it leaves significant
room for misapplication by implementers who may fail to consider the
latent data relationships that are required to be made explicit during
operationalization. Luckily, there are hundreds of reference taxonomies
for specific domains within data privacy published by academic
researchers, international standards organizations, and implicitly within
commercial products. While these often have higher normalization and
robustness due to their necessity for applied use, interoperability in
adjacent processes is almost never addressed within these but remains an
eventuality organizations must face as they seek to improve maturity,
where these organizations design and operate internal controls within
these privacy domains in their efforts to comply with increasingly
stringent and detailed legal requirements. Ultimately, this is a problem
of semantic compatibility and inadequate contextualization with increase
of scope, where disconnected taxonomies produce inconsistent terminology
and data, duplication without clear authority, and unrecognized or
unclear relationships. As a result, organizations meeting this profile
often rely on the subsequent data to draw conclusions in contexts where
that data is not sufficiently precise, normalized, or complete for the
purpose. This creates both operational and legal compliance challenges
such as exceeding purpose specification which erodes lawful bases of
processing, inadequate recognition and fulfillment of individual rights,
and missed opportunity through poor application of legal requirements.
 \\\\
Altogether, this highlights a condition faced by almost all large
organizations today, where the volume and importance of privacy-relevant
data is increasing based on external growth and internal investment
within the domain, but there is an industry-wide maturity gap where
fragmented and tool-bound processes lead to fragmented data faster than
organizations can normalize the data relationships to make control
capabilities legally reliable and defensible. In summary, the problem is
created at the intersection of all the following conditions:
 
\begin{itemize}
  \item External Conditions
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Large organizations tend to be bound by many regulatory schemes in the data privacy domain.
    \item Regulation largely does not codify the data relationships of the concepts they seek to govern.
  \end{itemize}

  \item Internal Conditions
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Organizations make many decisions during operationalization of privacy requirements that create data relationships, many of these decisions are implicit or the result of tool-binding.
    \item As these organizations mature processes that generate privacy regulatory data, the data relationships rarely hold true and frequently introduce various classes of normalization problems.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Solution Statement}

I propose there is a reference data model that provides a framework for willing organizations further clarity to approach this problem. The deliverables are anticipated as follows:
 
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A Reference (Data) Model} -- As documented primarily through an interactive browser-based presentation that allows for
  interaction with the model in a user-friendly format (e.g.\ GitHub
  pages explorer). Functionally, this will be the product of the
  compilation, technical abstraction, and analytical cross-mapping of
  existing ontologies, frameworks, taxonomies, and reference data
  models/schemas.
\item \textbf{A Report} -- I would like to share this work
  externally through a pre-print/publication style paper that
  contextualizes the problem and need, highlights the
  application considerations and design philosophy, introduces the solution, and provides suggestions for areas for
  further inquiry. I may also feed this into the W3C DPV Project or use
  it for a future contribution to their own implementation guides, which
  themselves are only domain specific.
\end{itemize}
\\
\\
This is a complex problem addressable only at the organization level that
ultimately relies on responsible individuals implementing organizational
data models with appropriate contextualization, there is no bypass of
this. The approach represents a distillation of common
structurally-grounded issues and considerations in an accessible place,
with the hope that they may be more readily found and applied by
practitioners. The idea is that there is value in the DPV as a framework
to interconnect otherwise disparate domain taxonomies and a forced
mapping of a high-quality subset of the hundreds of these would yield
considerations, decision points, and conflicts that the DPV is largely
silent on from the implementer perspective. Thus, if these are known and
can be made explicit, errors in the application of data models can be
reduced through planning and decision making during organizational
implementations.

\section*{Methodology Paragraph Summary}
 
The Reference Data Model was produced through a phased approach where each phase delivered an input to the subsequent phase. This allowed for phases to increase abstraction over the project and mitigated risks that would have arisen from premature optimization. At a high level, the methodology consists of the following milestones:
 
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Identified logical subdomains of the DPV and explicitly defined boundaries between subdomains.
  \item Identified taxonomies that relate to one or more of each subdomain.
  \item Evaluate the taxonomies per subdomain to select around the top 3 for each domain, based on both authoritativeness and detail/completeness. Retain these, shed the rest.
  \item For each subdomain; enumerate all data relationships implied through the top taxonomies.
  \item For each population of analysis within each domain; remove duplicates, reconcile conflicts, and normalize to at least a target normalization level.
  \item Structured analysis using various ontology tools and libraries to identify issues previously unknown, resolution of those issues. Limited stakeholder feedback raised to the W3C Working Group via Issue submission for high-confidence problems.
  \item Highlight and explore root causes of conflicts and normalization issues. This becomes the content of the report.
  \item Document via report and WebApp.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Evaluation}
The evaluation was composed to two distinct sets of activities, one focused on internal validity of the data model and the other as an early proxy for external validity.

For internal validity, the data model was declared as a turtle-compatible RDF ontology. After this, 100 test cases were created, derived from the NIST Privacy Workforce Taxonomy and the IAPP's Privacy Operational Lifecycle. These test cases are representative of common tasks performed within organizations related to data privacy, where the sum of the tasks is a good representative of what occurs at the program level within any organization. Each test case contained a plain language action that was translated to a SparQL query. A python schema validator was used, built on the \textit{rdflib} library to check for missing entities, format gaps, missing relationships, attribute and typing gaps, domain mismatches, and output range problem. Issues were iteratively reconciled over 12 runs, with a small number of final issues reserved as decisions.

For external validity, issues/perceived gaps in the W3C DPV that surfaced during analysis were submitted to the appropriate W3C working group for qualitative feedback. This serves as a kind of proxy for feedback for the privacy reference model, where these gaps became intermediate decisions needed for my work. My work surfaced 13 gaps, of which, 10 were already known in some form to the working group and three were not yet documented that I can tell. I do not know if I will hear back on these by the end of the course.

\subsection{Defining the scope and gathering the sources}

\subsubsection{Identifying the thirteen program-area domains}
The DPV, as an ontology, does not consider object attributes, typing, or keys. As a result, it must be prepared to receive these during operationalization. This requires the creation of boundaries, that ultimately form the domains of related concepts that define the taxonomy search. The number of two way cross-boundary relationships should be minimized. The model leans heavily on the DPV's module structure to define its sub-domains representing the process areas within a privacy program in a target organization. However, merely decomposing the DPV's module structure would have left common operational processes and artifacts without a home; including but not limited to Notice, Incident, and Regulatory Filing, as these are not first-class DPV modules. This resulted in a thirteen domain structure.

\subsection{Building the model}

\subsubsection{Defining recommended and optional relationships}
In performing the mapping, it was clear that not all relationships or even entities should be weighted equally in their importance. I assert that there is a "clean core" of relationships that should only be modified at the implementers peril, where such a modification represents a misunderstanding of the underlying concept or will come at the cost of an undercutting of the intended normalization, creating one or more downstream data quality problems which could negatively impact application. Recognizing this distinction between required and recommended, which are functionally the same in this model, versus optional, serves to create the space for maturity-keying and regulation-specific overlays. As a result, the model's design reflects this with mandatory recommendations for 34 core entities, 65 relationships, and 12 reified associations. Maturity-keyed peripheries and specific regulatory profiles can then be overlaid on these to extend the model once the "clean core" is in place.

\subsubsection{Keeping the core regulation-agnostic and attaching regulations as overlays}
The "clean core" was held to be strictly regulation-agnostic, where the requirements are addressed through a combination of abstraction, typing of entity typing, and regulation-specific extensions. This is a key requirement and reflects common organizational pain points from adoption of regulation-biased data models, sometimes implicitly. Generally, this reflects an intentional design principle of the data model reflecting that target organizations operate across multiple jurisdictions and may enter new jurisdictions, where bypassing the regulation-agnostic core for short term convenience produces issues of regime-coupling that is at odds with a core functional requirement the data model seeks to prevent.

\subsubsection{Layering structure, normalization, and complexity only at higher maturity levels}

There was some acknowledgment that complex concepts like versioning, the immutability of histories, temporal validity, edge provenance, and attestation introduce unnecessary complexities within the lowest maturity levels (L1, L2) that could threaten early adoption. As a result, the maturity structure contemplates these features separately and introduces them as nine possible extensions between L3 and L5 in a way that minimizes rework points. Organizations that advance their maturity should expect to add these peripheral structures to their "clean core", which implies incorporation of the necessary rework points. This could seemingly allow for investment to be articulated and planned against a "delta" map versus reverse engineereed against past program failures.

\section{Analysis, Observations, and Evaluation}
    This is a complex problem addressable only at the organization level that ultimately relies on responsible individuals implementing organizational data models with appropriate contextualization, there is no bypass of this. The approach represents a distillation of common structurally-grounded issues and considerations in an accessible place, with the hope that they may be more readily found and applied by practitioners. The idea is that there is value in the DPV as a framework to interconnect otherwise disparate domain taxonomies and a forced mapping of a high-quality subset of the hundreds of these would yield considerations, decision points, and conflicts that the DPV is largely silent on from the implementer perspective. Thus, if these are known and can be made explicit, errors in the application of data models can be reduced through planning and decision making during organizational implementations.

\subsection{What operating a privacy program actually requires}

\subsubsection{The structure needed for implementation far exceeds the vocabulary within the DPV}
\label{411}
The DPV is scoped as a controlled vocabulary of concepts with relationships, not as a schema. While expected, my analysis sustained this idea with the reference model containing 34 core entities, 65 core relationships, 12 reified-association classes, 51 per-domain entities, 37 per-domain relationships, and 87 cross-domain joins. This is roughly an order of magnitude more complex than the DPV and suggests that implementers should not treat DPV adoption as sufficient for operating a privacy program. Whether this reference model is used or another is originated, it should not be skipped to avoid the failure mode of "we did the DPV and still don't have a working operating model."

\subsubsection{subdomains have inconsistent amounts of prior art}
When examined per subdomain, the density of authoritative data relationships also varies by an order of magnitude. The output of standards bodies as well as the attention of regulators have inherent concentration within some areas including risk assessment methodologies (e.g. PIA, DPIA, FRIA, etc.), security controls, and consent record management. Inversely, incident and breach handling, notice management and transparency mechanisms, and technology an AI governance (an in-progress DPV extension) have the thinnest distribution of relationships. This distribution may not reflect the importance that the organization attributes to each area, implementers should expect different mixes of internal origination to support this through internal data architecture work. 

Metaanalysis of issues and considerations show common themes, including primary-entity selection, categories vs instances, granularity, reification depth, role-as-type vs role-as-relation, and theme-as-type vs theme-as-attribute decisions. A privacy program that adopts multiple sources without an explicit reconciliation step inherits these disagreements as latent inconsistencies. The reconciliation work has to happen somewhere; doing it during architecture is materially cheaper than discovering it during audit.

\subsubsection{Authoritative sources disagree, especially on foundational concepts that may help compose the "clean core" }
Even among sources with the highest authoritativeness, disagreement on cardinality, lifecycle, primary-entity choice, and within certain taxonomies showed disagreement. This is a reflection that each input taxonomy was authored to satisfy only its own scope. There were 103 documented source disagreements that were captured.

\subsection{Privacy regulations are doing the same thing in different words...mostly}

\subsubsection{Every regulation effectively implies a similar operational backbone}

When regulation profiles were layered in, which included GDPR, CCPA/CPRA, LGPD, and DPDPA for this exercise, there was limited divergence from the core relationships. Apparently, where divergence existed, it was more a reflection of the cultural and political framing in each regulatory regime, as opposed to new approaches to encode the operational reality of who is processing which data, for what purpose, under which legal authority. Further, five invariant classes (\texttt{ProcessingActivity}, \texttt{Purpose}, \texttt{PersonalDataCategory}, \texttt{LegalBasis}, \texttt{PartyRoleInActivity}) appear in every conformant implementation, at every maturity level, under every supported profile. This sustains the fundamental value idea and value proposition behind the reference implementation for organizations that have at least two differing regulatory obligations and further support the idea that the costs of implementing a common data model can be offset as regulatory complexity increases.
